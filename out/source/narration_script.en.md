## 6.1 Introduction

**s02.u01** `heading` e0002

Chapter six lines and angles.

**s02.u02** `heading` e0003

Six point one introduction.

**s02.u03** `explanation` e0004

In Chapter five you learned that at least two points are needed to draw a line. You also studied some axioms and used them to prove other statements. In this chapter you will study the properties of the angles formed when two lines intersect each other, and also the properties of the angles formed when a line intersects two or more parallel lines at distinct points. You will use these properties to prove statements by deductive reasoning. You have already verified these statements through activities in earlier classes.

**s02.u04** `explanation` e0005

In daily life you see different types of angles formed between the edges of plane surfaces. To make a model using plane surfaces you need a thorough knowledge of angles. For instance, suppose you want to make a model of a hut for a school exhibition using bamboo sticks. You would keep some sticks parallel and some sticks slanted. An architect drawing a plan for a multistoried building also draws intersecting lines and parallel lines at different angles. Without knowledge of the properties of these lines and angles, she could not draw the layout of the building.

**s02.u05** `explanation` e0006, e0007

Now, let us see why lines and angles are so important. In science we study light by drawing ray diagrams. When light passes from one medium to another we use the properties of intersecting lines and parallel lines to understand refraction. Similarly, when several forces act on a body we draw directed line segments to find the net effect. In such diagrams we must know how angles relate when the rays or line segments are parallel or intersect each other. To find the height of a tower or the distance of a ship from a lighthouse we use the angle between the horizontal and the line of sight. Many other situations use lines and angles. In the next chapters of geometry you will use these properties to deduce more useful results. Let us first revise the terms and definitions related to lines and angles that you learned in earlier classes.

You have studied different types of angles such as acute angle, right angle, obtuse angle, straight angle and reflex angle in earlier classes. Look at Figure six point one.

**s03.u06** `explanation` e0019

An acute angle measures between zero degrees and ninety degrees. A right angle is exactly ninety degrees. An angle greater than ninety degrees but less than one hundred and eighty degrees is called an obtuse angle. A straight angle is equal to one hundred and eighty degrees. An angle greater than one hundred and eighty degrees but less than three hundred and sixty degrees is called a reflex angle. Two angles whose sum is ninety degrees are called complementary angles, and two angles whose sum is one hundred and eighty degrees are called supplementary angles.

**s03.u07** `explanation` e0020

Two angles are adjacent if they have a common vertex, a common arm and their non common arms are on different sides of the common arm. In Figure six point two, angle A B D and angle D B C are adjacent angles. Ray B D is their common arm and point B is their common vertex. Ray B A and ray B C are non common arms. Moreover, when two angles are adjacent, then their sum is always equal to the angle formed by the two non common arms.
